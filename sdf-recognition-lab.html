Analysis by synthesis. One object is rendered at a hidden orientation drawn uniformly from SO(3), then blurred and occluded. That degraded image is the observation. To recognize it, the lab re-renders every shape across a grid that covers the whole rotation group and keeps the closest match, then refines the winner locally. In-plane roll costs no renders at all, because rolling the camera about its own axis only spins the image. There is no training here, only a fast renderer and a search — step by step, why that is possible.

There is no training here. Step by step, why that is possible.
  1. The problem is stated forwards, not backwards. A classifier learns p(label | image) from examples, because nobody can write that function down. Here the opposite direction is known exactly: give me a shape and an orientation and I will give you the image. Recognition is inverting a function we already have, not learning one we don't.
  2. That forward function cost nothing to obtain. Every shape on this page is a closed-form distance equation — the chair is a seat, a back and four legs under a min(), about six lines. Nothing was fitted to anything, so there is no dataset to collect and no weights to store.
  3. The unknowns are small even though the image is not. The observation is 64×64, so 4,096 numbers. The answer is one of five identities plus a point in SO(3), which is three numbers. A network would spend its capacity learning a map across the big space; the answer never needed more than four dimensions.
  4. So the search can be exhaustive instead of learned. Cover the rotation group with a grid, render every cell, keep the closest. Learning earns its keep when the forward model is unknown or too slow to run at query time. Here it is neither, so the usual reason to train never arises.
  5. One symmetry is what makes the grid affordable. Rolling the camera about its own view axis only spins the resulting image, so a single render can stand in for every roll of that view if you spin the observation to meet it. That turns 2,560 orientations per shape into 160 renders. Without this step the grid would be sixteen times the cost and the whole approach would stop being interactive. Cryo-EM handles in-plane rotation the same way, for the same reason.
  6. The comparison needs no learned features either. The renderer emits the same kind of picture as the observation, so the two can be subtracted directly — mean absolute difference over the union of the silhouettes. There is no embedding to train because there is no gap in representation to bridge.
  7. What gets paid instead. Compute at query time: about a thousand renders per answer, here, rather than one forward pass. And two assumptions — that the shape library is complete, and that the renderer is faithful to whatever made the observation. Adding a sixth shape means writing an equation, not gathering data; but an object outside the library cannot be recognized at all, only mis-assigned to the nearest thing that is.
  8. How it fails, and how you can tell. Under heavy occlusion it lands in the wrong pose basin: right shape, wrong orientation. The verdict below says so in yellow when it happens, and the search space above is labelled with each shape's measured look-alike rate. Both of those are read straight off the same renderer — being able to point at the reason for a mistake is the other thing you get for not training.
The honest boundary: this works because the world was authored. Real recognition problems rarely hand you the generator, and when they don't, learning one from examples is exactly what you are buying.
